Airport and station wayfinding: designing for travellers in a hurry

What makes wayfinding work in airports and railway stations: time pressure, language, accessibility and the places travellers need most.

AK
Anas KhanFounder, Areyos
17 Oct 20265 min readVenues

On this page

    Key takeaways

    • Travellers are time-pressured, so directions must be instant and unambiguous.
    • Gates, check-in, baggage and transport are the core destinations.
    • Language and iconography matter as much as the map.
    • Digital directions complement, never replace, official signage.

    A traveller in an airport or station is solving a deadline, not exploring. They want to know three things: where do I go, how far is it, and how long will it take?

    Core destinations

    • Check-in, security and boarding gates or platforms.
    • Baggage reclaim and exits.
    • Taxis, metro, buses and parking.
    • Lounges, food, toilets, prayer rooms and help desks.

    Design for pressure

    • Short, consistent labels. The same name on every sign and screen.
    • Clear distance and time cues, such as “gate 12, about 8 minutes”.
    • Multiple languages and icons so no one is stranded by wording.
    • Step-free routes for wheelchairs, prams and heavy luggage.

    Where digital directions help

    Official signs and announcements remain the primary guide. QR directions can fill the gaps: connecting terminals, reaching a far gate, or finding a specific lounge. They are most useful where signage is dense and visitors are unfamiliar. For how it works, see QR navigation without an app.

    Regulation and safety

    Airports and stations operate under strict rules for safety signage and security areas. Any visitor wayfinding must be agreed with the operator and must never conflict with official signs.

    A note on scope. Our early focus is hospitals, malls and campuses. We include airports because the same principles apply across large public venues.
