QR code or app? Why most venues should start with a scan

Indoor navigation apps sound impressive, but visitors rarely install them. A look at when a QR code is the better answer, and when it is not.

AK
Anas KhanFounder, Areyos
14 Oct 20265 min readGuides

On this page

    Key takeaways

    • Install friction is the main reason apps fail for one-time visitors.
    • QR works on any phone with a camera, with nothing to download or update.
    • Apps still win for daily users, offline use and AR overlays.
    • Many venues start with QR and add an app only if usage proves it is needed.

    The first idea in most indoor navigation pitches is an app. It feels like the serious option. But a navigation tool only helps if visitors actually use it, and the hardest step is the first one: getting it onto the phone.

    The install problem

    Think about the person you are trying to help. They are in a hospital corridor, a mall they visit twice a year, or an airport hall with a gate closing. Asking them to find an app store listing, wait for a download, grant permissions and create an account is asking too much at the wrong moment.

    A QR code asks for one thing: point the camera. The route opens in the browser they already have.

    QR vs app at a glance

    QR on the wallDedicated app
    First-use effortScan, choose, goFind, install, open, allow permissions
    Works for one-time visitorsYesRarely
    Hardware to installNone, just printed codesOften none, sometimes beacons
    Updates when a room movesEdit the map, codes stayEdit the map and ship an update
    Offline useNeeds a connectionCan work offline
    Best forVisitors, patients, shoppersDaily users, staff, AR experiences

    When an app still makes sense

    • Frequent users. Staff or students who navigate the same campus daily may prefer an installed tool.
    • Weak connectivity. Deep basements and shielded wards may need offline maps.
    • Rich features. Live location dots and AR arrows need app-level sensors.

    A sensible path

    Start with QR. It is fast to launch and cheap to correct; here is a one-week rollout plan and what to budget. Watch which destinations people choose and where they scan. If a group of users clearly needs more, add an app for them later, using the same floor plan.

    Honest trade-off. QR navigation shows the route to follow, not a live blue dot. For most visitor journeys that is enough, but it is a real difference worth knowing.
